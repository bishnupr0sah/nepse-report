§Abstract

We estimate the fundamental value of the NEPSE equity market from the bottom up, using 282 operating companies across 12 sectors. Reported earnings are normalised by mean-reverting each company’s return on equity toward its sector’s through-cycle level, with additional discounting of one-off earnings shocks and credit-cost adjustments for banks. Company values are derived from sector-appropriate models — residual income, justified price-to-book, two-stage dividend discount, free cash flow to the firm, licence-bound project cash flows, appraisal value and sum-of-the-parts — under a build-up cost of equity that adds sector and liquidity premia to a Damodaran-based country risk premium. At the market level we anchor valuation to NEPSE’s own cycle multiples (12.3× trough, 18.5× cycle-normal, 42.3× 2021 peak) and compare it with global, emerging, frontier and macro-peer markets. We find that NEPSE trades at 36.9× reported and 31.3× normalised earnings, within 13% of its 2021 peak multiple and above developed-market levels, while normalised return on equity is 8.7%. The market-implied cost of equity is about 6.4%, against a bottom-up estimate of 13.8%. A cycle-normal multiple with a 20–30% structural “Nepal premium” implies an index range of about 1,860–2,015. For today’s level to be consistent with that range within three years, aggregate earnings would need to compound at 9–19% a year with the index flat, against about 2.6% a year since 2021. We discuss the structural reasons for the premium, the conditions under which it could persist, and the limitations of the method.

Key findings

  1. Valuation level. Market P/E is 36.9× (31.3× normalised) and P/B 2.71×, against a 2019 trough of 12.3× and 1.78× and a 2021 peak of 42.3×. The current multiple is 87% of the 2021 peak.
  2. Supply, not earnings, explains market-cap growth. Market capitalisation per index point is about 20% higher than in 2021, while index-level earnings are about 6% lower. New listings, mostly hydropower, added value at high multiples and low returns.
  3. Breadth is weaker than the headline. Excluding commercial banks, the market trades at about 55× earnings; 182 of 282 stocks sit above the 2021 peak multiple on normalised earnings.
  4. Global position. NEPSE’s multiple exceeds developed markets (23.0×), emerging markets (16.0×) and MSCI Frontier (13.3×), and is three to five times the level of macro peers such as Pakistan, Bangladesh and Kenya.
  5. Fair-value range. At 18.5× normalised earnings the index-equivalent is about 1,550; with a 20–30% structural premium, 1,860–2,015. Today embeds a 69% premium on normalised (100% on reported) earnings.
  6. Required growth. To validate 2,623 at a 30% premium in three years requires 9.2% a year earnings growth with the index flat (normalised basis), or 21.2% a year if the index is also to deliver an 11% annual price return.

1Introduction and research questions

NEPSE closed at 2,623.56 on 23 November 2026, about 18% below its all-time high of 3,198.60 (18 August 2021). Market capitalisation, however, has exceeded its 2021 level, reaching a record of about Rs 5 trillion in July 2025 and Rs 4.83 trillion (73% of GDP, 294 companies) in mid-April 2026. The divergence between the index and aggregate market value, and the return of the market multiple to near-record levels, motivate four questions:

  1. How is the NEPSE market valued today, on reported and on normalised earnings?
  2. Why have valuations returned close to the 2021 peak, despite many new listings?
  3. How does NEPSE compare with its own past cycles, with global markets and with economies at a similar stage of development?
  4. What earnings path would be needed over the next three years for the current index level to be consistent with fundamental value?

The paper is structured as follows. Sections 2–6 describe the data, normalisation procedure, cost of capital and company-level models. Sections 7–10 place the market in historical and international context. Sections 11–13 derive fair-value ranges, required growth and scenarios. Sections 14–16 discuss risks, limitations and conclusions.

2Data and sample construction

2.1 Universe

The sample comprises 282 NEPSE-listed operating companies as of 23 November 2026. Mutual funds, debentures and preference shares are excluded because their values are not driven by operating earnings. Company data (price, shares outstanding, face value, promoter holding, EPS, book value per share, ROE, ROA, operating margin, leverage, dividends, and sector-specific metrics such as NPL, net interest margin, CD ratio, solvency and loss ratios) were collected from the company pages of nepselabs.com, which compile the latest published quarterly or annual statements.

2.2 Sector classification

Companies are grouped into NEPSE’s twelve operating sectors: commercial banks (19), development banks (16), finance (15), microfinance (50), life insurance (14), non-life insurance (13), hydropower (111), manufacturing and processing (18), hotels and tourism (8), investment (7), trading (2) and others (9).

2.3 Data validation

Each record passes automated consistency checks before valuation: payout ratios above 100% of earnings, price-to-book ratios inconsistent with price and book value, combined ratios below loss ratios for non-life insurers, missing or negative book value, and EPS swings above 100% year on year. Flagged items were reviewed; where a source field was evidently erroneous (for example, a reported payout of 5,729%), the derived figure is recomputed from primary inputs. The aggregate reported P/E (36.95×) and P/B (2.71×) are consistent with independent market-level estimates published by Sharesansar (38.3× and 2.78× in late 2025), allowing for date and sample differences.

2.4 Units and conventions

Monetary values are in Nepalese rupees; 1 Arba = Rs 1 billion; 1 crore = Rs 10 million. Ratios are expressed as multiples (×) or percentages. All market aggregates are capitalisation-weighted, computed as ratios of sums rather than averages of ratios (Section 3).

3Market aggregates

Aggregate multiples are computed as the ratio of summed market value to summed fundamentals. This is equivalent to a capitalisation-weighted harmonic mean of company multiples and avoids the upward bias from loss-making or near-zero-earnings companies that afflicts simple averages.

P/Emkt =Σi Pi · NiΣi EPSi · Ni,   P/Bmkt =Σi Pi · NiΣi BVPSi · Ni(1)

where Pi is the share price, Ni shares outstanding, EPSi trailing earnings per share and BVPSi book value per share. The normalised P/E substitutes normalised EPS (Section 4). Aggregate ROE is Σ earnings ÷ Σ book value.

4Earnings normalisation

Reported earnings in Nepal are volatile for reasons unrelated to sustainable profitability: loan-loss provision charges and write-backs under changing NRB rules, one-off investment gains, regulatory dividend restrictions, and the commissioning phase of hydropower projects. Valuing a company on one year’s reported EPS can therefore materially misstate its value. For example, Kumari Bank’s reported EPS rose about 249% in one year largely because a Rs 3.45 Arba provision charge turned into a Rs 1.44 Arba write-back; on normalised earnings its EPS is about Rs 17.6 rather than the reported figure.

4.1 Return-on-equity mean reversion

Normalised ROE is a weighted average of reported ROE and the sector’s through-cycle ROE, with a lower weight on the reported figure when earnings show signs of a shock:

ROENi = w · ROERi + (1 − w) · ROETCs − δi(2)
w = 0.15 if shocki, otherwise 0.50;   shocki ⇔ |ΔEPS| > 100% ∨ ROER < 0 ∨ ROER > 2 · ROETCs(3)

where ROETCs is the through-cycle ROE of sector s (Appendix A) and δi is a credit-cost drag for banks and financial institutions.

4.2 Credit-cost adjustment

For banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions, non-performing loans above 4% signal that current provisioning may understate future credit costs:

δi = 0.5 · max(0, NPLi − 4%)(4)

i.e. each percentage point of NPL above 4% reduces normalised ROE by 0.5 percentage points. Normalised ROE is floored at zero.

4.3 Normalised EPS and near-term growth

EPSNi = ROENi · BVPSi;   g1 = max[2%, min(gcaps, ROENi · (1 − bs))](5)

where bs is the sector’s normal payout ratio and gcaps a five-year nominal growth cap. Book value is used as the anchor because it is far more stable than earnings and, for financial companies, is the regulatory capital base. Using book value assumes reported equity is fairly stated; companies with large unrecognised losses would be overvalued by this step.

4.4 Effect on the market

Normalisation lowers the market P/E from 36.95× to 31.32×, because aggregate normalised earnings (about Rs 142 Arba) exceed reported earnings: several sectors — notably hydropower, non-life and life insurance, and investment companies — reported earnings below their through-cycle levels. In microfinance, finance and development banks, normalisation raises the multiple because recent earnings were above sustainable levels or NPLs are elevated.

5Cost of equity

5.1 Build-up model

We use a capital asset pricing model extended for country, sector and liquidity risk:

Ke,i = Rf + βs · ERPNepal + SRPs + LPi(6)

where Rf is the risk-free rate, βs a sector beta, ERP the equity risk premium for Nepal, SRPs a sector-specific premium for risks not captured by beta (regulatory, hydrological, concentration), and LPi a company liquidity premium.

5.2 Risk-free rate and equity risk premium

We adopt Damodaran’s country risk framework (latest available update) for a consistent, internationally comparable discount rate:

ERPNepal = ERPmature + CRPNepal = 4.23% + 4.66% = 8.89%(7)
CRP = Default spreadrating ×σequityσgov. bond(8)

The default spread corresponds to Nepal’s sovereign rating (Moody’s Ba3; Fitch BB−). The rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee and the analysis uses a USD-equivalent risk-free rate of 2.94% for consistency with the Damodaran ERP. A local-currency alternative (Nepal government bond yields of about 3% and NRB’s 4.5% policy rate) gives similar results because Nepal’s nominal yields are currently close to USD levels. The tool allows the user to switch between this default and alternative rate sets.

5.3 Liquidity premium

5.4 Worked examples

A large commercial bank: 2.94% + 0.90 × 8.89% + 0 + 0 = 10.94%. A small hydropower company with Rs 2 Arba free float: 2.94% + 1.20 × 8.89% + 1.0% + 2.0% = 16.61%. The capitalisation-weighted market cost of equity is 13.83%; sector values appear in Exhibit 2.

Interpretation. These are required returns for a globally diversified investor pricing Nepal’s risk. Domestic investors face a closed capital account and few alternatives, so the return they accept in practice is lower (Section 10). This gap is the single most important judgment in valuing NEPSE.

6Company valuation models

Each company is valued with two to four models suited to its sector, then blended with sector weights (Appendix A). All models use normalised inputs by default. Long-term growth gT is 5% nominal (4% for “others”, 0% for licence-bound hydropower).

6.1 Multi-stage residual income model (banks, insurers, financials)

Value equals current book value plus the present value of future earnings in excess of the cost of equity. ROE fades linearly from its normalised level to its long-term level over n = 5 years; thereafter residual income decays at persistence rate ω = 0.7:

V0 = B0 + Σt=1n(ROEt − Ke) · Bt−1(1 + Ke)t+(ROELT − Ke) · Bn(1 + Ke − ω)(1 + Ke)n(9)

with Bt = Bt−1(1 + ROEt(1 − payout)). Residual income is preferred for financial companies because it anchors on regulatory book value and is less sensitive to terminal assumptions than a DCF.

6.2 Justified price-to-book (Gordon form)

V0 = B0 ·ROE − gTKe − gT(10)

A company earning exactly its cost of equity is worth book value; the model is undefined when Ke ≤ gT.

6.3 Two-stage dividend discount model

V0 = Σt=1nEPS0(1 + g1)t · p1(1 + Ke)t+EPSn(1 + gT) · pT(Ke − gT)(1 + Ke)n,  pT = 1 −gTROELT(11)

The terminal payout is set consistently with sustainable growth, so that growth is funded by retained earnings at the long-term ROE. First-stage payout p1 is the current cash payout, floored at 10%. Bonus shares are not treated as dividends; they change share count but not value.

6.4 Free cash flow to the firm (manufacturing, hotels, trading, others)

FCFFt = Revt[m(1 − τ) + d − c] − w(Revt − Revt−1)(12)
WACC =EE + DKe +DE + DKd(1 − τ);   Vequity = ΣFCFFt(1+WACC)t+ TV − Debt + Cash(13)

m is EBIT margin, d and c depreciation and capital expenditure as shares of revenue, w net working capital intensity, τ tax rate. Revenue growth fades linearly from g1 to gT over five years. Terminal reinvestment is set to maintenance capex plus the growth component, avoiding the common error of a terminal value that grows without reinvestment.

6.5 Licence-bound project FCFE (hydropower)

Hydropower companies operate under generation licences of typically 30–35 years, after which assets revert to the Government of Nepal. A perpetuity model is therefore inappropriate. We discount free cash flow to equity until licence expiry with zero terminal value:

V0 = Σt=1LNIt + Dept − Capext − Principalt(1 + Ke)t+ Cash0(14)

Revenue = design energy × plant availability × PPA tariff (with contractual escalation); royalties combine a capacity charge per MW and an energy royalty that steps up after year 15; tax holidays and half-rate periods follow the Electricity Act; debt amortises over the loan tenor. Minimum debt-service cover is reported as a solvency check. Where project-level inputs are not available, the model falls back to the generic equity models in 6.1–6.3, which is the case for most companies in this market-level study.

6.6 Appraisal value (life insurance)

V = ANW + VIF + k · VNB;   VIF ≈ EPSN ·1 − (1 + Ke)−nKe(15)

Adjusted net worth (book value) plus the value of in-force business plus a multiple of new-business value. Nepali insurers do not publish embedded value, so VIF is proxied as an annuity of normalised shareholder profit over a 10-year run-off; VNB is excluded unless supplied.

6.7 Normalised underwriting ROE (non-life insurance)

ROENL =[NEP · (1 − CRnorm) + IA · y] · (1 − τ)Equity(16)

Net earned premium times one minus the normalised combined ratio, plus investment assets times yield. This ROE then drives the residual income and justified P/B models.

6.8 Sum-of-the-parts NAV (investment companies)

NAV = MVlisted + a · BVunlisted + Loans + Cash + Other − Liabilities − tcg · max(0, MV − BV)listed;  V = NAV(1 − h)(17)

Deferred capital gains tax at 25% is deducted on unrealised gains, and a 20% holding-company discount h reflects illiquidity, costs and governance. Because listed holdings are themselves marked at NEPSE prices, NAV inherits market overvaluation.

6.9 Cycle-normal relative valuation

Vrel = EPSN × 18.5;   VEV/EBITDA = EBITDA × Ms − Debt + Cash;   VEV/MW = Bench × MW − Debt + Cash(18)

Relative models use NEPSE’s own cycle-normal multiple (Section 7) rather than current peer multiples, which would only reproduce prevailing market levels.

6.10 Blending, dispersion and fallback

V* =Σk wk Vk · 𝟙(Vk > 0)Σk wk · 𝟙(Vk > 0);   CV =σ(Vk)μ(Vk)(19)

Models returning missing or non-positive values are excluded and weights re-scaled. The coefficient of variation across models is reported as a confidence measure. If a sector’s primary model lacks inputs (for example project data for hydropower), weights default to residual income 40%, justified P/B 25%, DDM 20%, relative P/E 15%. A Graham number √(22.5 · EPS · BVPS) is shown for reference only.

6.11 Classification and reverse valuation

Each company is placed in one of five zones by comparing price P with blended value V* and cycle bands EPSN × {12.3, 42.3}: below cycle-low (P ≤ 12.3 · EPSN); below intrinsic (P ≤ 0.95V*); near intrinsic (±5%); above intrinsic within NEPSE’s historical range; and above the 2021 peak multiple (P ≥ 42.3 · EPSN). Reverse valuation solves for what the price implies:

Keimpl = g +ROE − gP/B;   ROEreq = g + P/B · (Ke − g);   gimpl =P · Ke − DP + D(20)

7.1 Choice of anchors

Trough (12.3×, P/B 1.78×): the February 2019 market low, consistent with the 2011/12 low. Cycle-normal (18.5×): set between the MSCI Frontier (13.3×) and broad emerging-market (16–19×) multiples, and close to the midpoint of NEPSE’s own trough-to-peak range on a log scale (√(12.3 × 42.3) ≈ 22.8× is the geometric midpoint; 18.5× is deliberately set below it because past peaks were liquidity-driven and transient). Peak (42.3×): the August 2021 high. Company-level cycle bands are EPSN × {12.3, 18.5, 42.3}.

Four mechanisms explain the pattern. (i) Supply at high multiples. Most listings since 2021 are hydropower companies; the sector now has 111 stocks at 78.6× reported earnings and a 7.6% normalised ROE (median ROE near 4%). These listings raise market value per index point without adding commensurate earnings. (ii) Stalled incumbent earnings. Banks absorbed heavy provisioning; insurers, microfinance and finance companies saw profits decline. (iii) Liquidity. NRB’s policy rate of 4.5%, development-bond yields near 3%, and a closed capital account channel remittance-driven savings into equities. (iv) Bonus-share illusion. Bonus issues raise share counts and dilute EPS, but are widely treated as returns, supporting demand without adding value.

Breadth confirms that the typical stock is more expensive than in 2021: commercial banks (27% of market value at 19.6×) hold the aggregate multiple down, while the remaining 73% trades at roughly 55× earnings.

Across frontier and emerging markets, valuation multiples rise with income, institutional depth and openness to foreign capital; market capitalisation relative to GDP tends to rise with income as well. Nepal inverts these regularities: the lowest income per head in the peer group, the highest multiple, the highest market cap to GDP and the lowest dividend yield. The premium is not explained by profitability (normalised ROE 8.7%) or risk (ERP 8.89% versus 7.08% for India and 4.23% for mature markets). The most plausible explanation is structural: a closed capital account, captive remittance-fed domestic liquidity, near-zero real deposit rates, limited real-sector listings and minimal foreign participation.

10Theoretical versus empirical anchors

For a market in steady state, the justified multiples follow from the sustainable-growth identity g = ROE(1 − payout):

P/E* =(1 − g/ROE)(1 + g)Ke − g;   P/B* =ROE − gKe − g(22)

At Ke = 13.8% and g = 5%, the justified P/B for ROE of 8.7% is 0.42×. No ROE can justify 31× at this cost of equity: the ceiling as ROE → ∞ is (1+g)/(Ke−g) = 11.9×.

Inverting equation (22) at today’s P/B of 2.71× and normalised ROE of 8.66% gives a market-implied cost of equity of about 6.4% (6.35% from P/B; 6.42% from the normalised P/E). Equivalently, at a 13.8% cost of equity the market would require a sustainable ROE of about 29% to justify its book multiple. Investors are therefore pricing NEPSE equities to return roughly what bank deposits and government bonds yield, with little or no risk premium.

NEPSE has never traded at these textbook values: even the 2019 trough was 1.78× book. This is why the paper uses two lenses. Company-level intrinsic values (Section 6) answer “what would a global investor pay?”, while market-level fair values (Section 11) answer “what has NEPSE itself sustained through cycles?”. The second lens is more relevant for the index; the first indicates how much of today’s price rests on Nepal-specific liquidity conditions.

11.1 Why allow a premium

A 20–30% premium is defensible while the structural conditions of Section 9 persist: captive liquidity, negative or near-zero real deposit rates, scarcity of investable listed assets, and limited outward investment options. The premium should be expected to compress if deposit rates rise to double digits, foreign portfolio access opens, or listing supply accelerates.

12.1 Feasibility

Aggregate earnings have grown about 2.6% a year since 2021. Nominal GDP growth targets are about 12.5% and private credit growth about 11% (NRB Monetary Policy 2083/84). Earnings growth can be decomposed into balance-sheet growth and changes in profitability:

1 + gE = (1 + gB) ×ROEtROEt−1(25)

With retention-funded book growth of roughly 4–6% a year, Test A at a 30% premium (9.2%) requires market ROE to rise from 8.7% to about 10% over three years, plausible if bank NPLs fall and hydropower projects reach full operation. Test B (21.2%) would require ROE to reach roughly 13–14%, a level not sustained by the market in the recent cycle. Validating today’s level with a normal equity return therefore rests on a combination of strong earnings recovery and the persistence of a large structural premium.

The scenario set shows an asymmetric fundamental profile: the central scenarios cluster around today’s level over three years, implying low prospective returns relative to the cost of equity; meaningful upside requires both strong earnings and the persistence of the current premium; and a return of the premium to historical cycle-normal would imply a substantial decline even with moderate earnings growth. History (Section 7) suggests the multiple, not earnings, has determined the direction of NEPSE at each turning point, and that liquidity conditions have been its leading indicator.

13.1 Indicators to monitor

Deposit and lending rates and the interbank rate; banking-system CD ratio and excess liquidity; margin-loan exposure and NRB directives; market turnover; NPL trends at commercial banks; commissioning and generation of hydropower projects and power-export volumes; IPO and FPO pipeline and pricing; and any change to foreign portfolio investment rules.

14Risks and catalysts

Risks to valuation

  • Rise in deposit and policy rates, removing the liquidity premium
  • Forced selling from margin loans in a correction
  • Provision write-backs inflating reported earnings temporarily
  • A pipeline of richly priced IPOs adding supply
  • Turnover decline (−69% year on year by March 2026) reducing depth
  • Hydrological and transmission risk in hydropower
  • Regulatory changes on dividends, capital and margin lending

Potential catalysts

  • Recovery in bank earnings as NPLs fall and credit grows
  • Hydropower projects reaching full operation and power exports to India and Bangladesh
  • Opening to foreign portfolio investment
  • More real-sector listings broadening the market
  • Sustained low interest rates and stable remittances
  • Improved disclosure, including embedded value for life insurers

15Limitations and robustness

  1. Index scaling. NEPSE is a capitalisation-weighted index adjusted for new listings; equation (23) assumes the index moves proportionally with aggregate market value at a constant earnings base. Index divisor adjustments and composition differences between the index and our 282-company sample mean the mapping is approximate.
  2. 2021 earnings. Earnings at the 2021 peak are estimated as market capitalisation ÷ P/E, not compiled bottom-up.
  3. Normalisation parameters. Through-cycle ROEs and shock weights are judgments. A ±1 pp change in all sector through-cycle ROEs changes normalised market earnings by roughly ±6–10%, and the fair index proportionally.
  4. Cost of equity. Company intrinsic values are highly sensitive to Ke; a ±1 pp change moves residual-income values by roughly 10–20%. Market fair values in Section 11 do not depend on Ke.
  5. Data quality. Source data are compiled by a third party from published statements and may contain errors or lags; automated checks reduce but do not eliminate this risk.
  6. Peer data. International multiples come from different providers, dates and methodologies and should be read as indicative.
  7. Hydropower. Project-level models were not populated for most companies; generic equity models were used, which may misstate value for projects in early operation.
  8. Structural change. Cycle anchors assume Nepal’s market structure is broadly stable. A permanent change, such as capital-account liberalisation, could shift fair multiples in either direction.

Robustness. Conclusions about the level of valuation are robust across bases: on reported earnings the premium over cycle-normal is 100%, on normalised earnings 69%; on P/B the market is 52% above its 2019 trough. The direction of the findings does not change across the 20–50% premium range.

16Conclusion

NEPSE’s current valuation is high by its own history, by international comparison and relative to the profitability of listed companies. The return of the multiple to near-2021 levels reflects new supply at high multiples, stalled earnings and abundant domestic liquidity, rather than a step change in returns on capital. The market is implicitly pricing equities to yield close to deposit rates. A structural premium is justified while Nepal’s capital account remains closed and liquidity remains abundant, and a 20–30% premium gives a model range of about 1,860–2,015. Consistency between today’s index and that range within three years would require aggregate earnings growth of 9–19% a year with a flat index, or substantially more if investors are to earn a normal return. Whether that occurs depends chiefly on bank earnings recovery, the maturation of hydropower, and above all the path of interest rates and liquidity. These conclusions describe fundamental value under stated assumptions; they do not predict the market’s path, which may diverge from fundamentals for extended periods.
